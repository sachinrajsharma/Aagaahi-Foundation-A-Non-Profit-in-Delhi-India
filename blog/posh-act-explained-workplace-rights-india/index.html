The POSH Act Explained: Your Rights and Duties at Work in India

By Aagahi Foundation · 14 November 2026 · 6 min read

The POSH Act is the law that protects women from sexual harassment at work in India, yet many employees and even employers have never read it. POSH stands for the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013. It tells every workplace what it must do before a complaint arrives, and what it must do after.

Aagahi Foundation runs POSH awareness training for professionals. This post explains the Act in plain language. It is general information, not legal advice, so for a real case please speak to a lawyer or your Internal Committee.

Where the POSH Act came from

Before 2013, workplaces followed guidelines set by the Supreme Court in the Vishaka case of 1997. Parliament later turned those guidelines into a full law. You can read the Act on the India Code website, and the Ministry of Women and Child Development publishes official guidance.

What counts as sexual harassment under the POSH Act

The Act describes sexual harassment as any unwelcome behaviour of a sexual nature. That includes:

  • physical contact and advances,
  • a demand or request for sexual favours,
  • sexually coloured remarks,
  • showing pornography, and
  • any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature.

The key word is "unwelcome". It does not matter if the person meant it as a joke. What matters is how it affects the woman who received it.

Who is covered, and where

The word "workplace" is wide. It covers offices, shops, hospitals, schools, NGOs and government departments. It also covers places an employee visits for work, including transport that the employer provides. Even a domestic worker can complain.

The word "employee" is wide as well. Regular staff, daily-wage workers, contract workers, interns, trainees and volunteers all count.

The Internal Committee: who sits on it and what it does

Every workplace with ten or more employees must set up an Internal Committee, or IC. Each office or branch needs its own. The Act sets out who sits on it:

  • a senior woman employee as the presiding officer,
  • at least two members who are committed to women's causes or who have social work or legal experience,
  • one external member from an NGO or association working for women, or a person familiar with the issue, and
  • at least half of the members must be women.

The IC receives complaints, holds an inquiry, and recommends action to the employer. It should complete the inquiry within 90 days, and the Act requires it to keep the identities of everyone involved confidential.

If there is no Internal Committee

Smaller workplaces, with fewer than ten employees, do not need an IC. Instead, the District Officer sets up a Local Committee in each district. A woman can also go to the Local Committee if her complaint is against the employer. Either way, no one is left without a route.

The Ministry has also launched an online portal called SHe-Box. It helps women find the right committee and file a complaint, and it is meant to keep a record of committees across the country. Check the portal for the latest rules about registering committees.

How to file a complaint, step by step

  1. Write it down. Note the dates, places, what happened and who saw it. Keep messages, emails and any other evidence.
  2. Submit a written complaint to the Internal Committee or the Local Committee. If you cannot write it yourself, the committee must help you put it in writing.
  3. Do it within three months of the incident. The committee can extend this by three months for a good reason.
  4. Take part in the inquiry. Both sides can present their case.
  5. Expect an outcome. If the committee finds the complaint proved, it recommends action under the employer's service rules, such as a warning, a withheld promotion or termination.

Fear of retaliation stops many women from speaking up. If someone threatens your job because you complained, tell the committee in writing and speak to a lawyer.

Myths about the POSH Act

  • "It only applies to big companies." It does not. A small office with fewer than ten employees falls under the Local Committee instead, so the law still applies.
  • "It is only about a boss and an employee." The law covers harassment by anyone at work, including colleagues, clients and visitors.
  • "A joke cannot count." A joke can count if it is sexual and unwelcome. Intent does not decide the matter.
  • "Complaining will end my career." Many women fear that, and the fear is real. That is why the Act demands confidentiality and gives the committee power to recommend interim relief, such as a transfer or leave, while the inquiry runs.

How to support a colleague who has been harassed

Often the first person who hears about it is not a manager but a friend. If that is you, a few simple steps help.

  1. Listen without judging, and believe what you hear.
  2. Do not push her to complain, and do not pressure her to stay silent either. The choice is hers.
  3. Offer to help her write down the facts and keep the evidence.
  4. Tell her about the Internal Committee, and offer to go with her.
  5. Keep what she told you private.

What employers must do

An employer has duties even when no one has complained. It must set up an IC, display the penalties for harassment, hold awareness programmes, and give the IC the support it needs. That is exactly why training matters. A committee that nobody knows about cannot help anyone.

Frequently asked questions about the POSH Act

Does the POSH Act protect men?

The Act protects women. Many employers also adopt gender-neutral policies, so ask your organisation about its own rules. A man who faces harassment may have other legal options, so it is wise to speak to a lawyer.

Does the Act cover interns, volunteers and contract staff?

Yes. The Act defines an employee broadly. It includes regular, temporary and daily-wage staff, contract workers, trainees, apprentices and people who work as volunteers, with or without pay.

How long do I have to file a complaint?

A written complaint should reach the committee within three months of the incident, or of the last incident in a series. The committee can extend that by another three months if it is satisfied that you had a good reason for the delay.

What if my workplace has no Internal Committee?

Employers with ten or more employees must have one. If yours does not, or you work for a smaller organisation, you can approach the Local Committee set up by the District Officer. The SHe-Box portal can also help you find the right committee.

Can a complaint be settled with money?

The Act allows conciliation, but only if the woman asks for it. It does not allow a money settlement as the basis for conciliation.
