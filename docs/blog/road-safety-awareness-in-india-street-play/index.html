Road Safety Awareness in India: Why We Take Street Plays to Busy Markets

By Aagahi Foundation · 14 November 2026 · 6 min read

Road safety awareness is one of the few topics where a few minutes of attention can decide whether someone gets home. India recorded 4,61,312 road accidents in 2022, and 1,68,491 people died in them, according to the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways. Those numbers are families, not statistics.

Aagahi Foundation runs street-play road safety drives in busy Delhi markets, together with traffic police. This post explains why we choose street theatre, what the data says, and the habits that protect you and the people you love.

What the road safety numbers tell us

The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways publishes the national figures each year. You can read its reports on the official MoRTH website. The 2022 report points to three things.

  • Riders pay the highest price. Two-wheeler users were the largest group among those killed, at roughly 75,000 people in 2022, according to press coverage of the report.
  • Speed is the biggest single cause. Overspeeding was linked to about seven in every ten road deaths.
  • Safety gear matters. A large number of the people killed were not wearing a helmet or a seat belt.

These figures come from the report as covered in the press, so please check the primary report before you quote an exact number.

Why we take road safety awareness to the street

A poster in a hallway does little, because people walk past it. A story in a busy market does more. When performers act out a near miss in costume and with humour, people stop. They watch because they are already waiting, and they remember the scene better than a rule.

Traffic police joined our drives in Delhi, and the press covered them. That matters, because commuters listen when the people who enforce the rules also explain the reasons.

Habits that protect riders

  1. Wear a BIS-standard helmet, and fasten the strap. A helmet on the head but not strapped offers very little protection.
  2. Insist that your pillion rider wears one too. The rule applies to them as well.
  3. Keep your speed within the limit, especially near schools, markets and crossings.
  4. Put your phone away. A text can wait. A crash cannot be undone.
  5. Never ride after drinking.

Drivers have their own list. Wear the seat belt in every seat, and secure children properly. Leave space between you and the vehicle ahead. Then slow down before a zebra crossing, not at it.

Habits that protect walkers and children

Pedestrians are not safe just because they are walking. Use the footpath and the zebra crossing wherever there is one. Stop at the kerb, look right, then left, then right again, and cross only when the road is clear. Teach children the same sequence, and practise it with them on a real road.

Never let a child run across the road to a parent or a friend. Hold hands near traffic until they can judge speed and distance themselves. That usually comes later than we expect.

The school run: small choices that matter

Every morning, millions of children travel to school on the back of a scooter or in a crowded vehicle. A few small choices lower the risk.

  • Never put three or more people on a two-wheeler. Overloaded bikes are hard to balance and brake.
  • Give a child a helmet that fits. An adult helmet slides around and protects little.
  • Seat young children in the back of a car, with a seat belt or a child restraint. Never hold a child on your lap.
  • Park a short walk away and let children step out on the footpath side, not into traffic.

How to start a road safety drive in your neighbourhood

You do not need a big budget to raise road safety awareness where you live. Start small and keep it simple.

  1. Pick one message, such as "wear your helmet" or "stop at the zebra crossing".
  2. Talk to the local traffic police and the market association first. Their support makes people listen.
  3. Keep the act short. Five minutes with a clear story and some humour holds a crowd better than a speech.
  4. End with one action. Ask everyone to try the habit this week, and thank them for stopping.

What to do if you see a crash

Most people hesitate because they fear trouble with the police. The law answers that fear directly. Section 134A of the Motor Vehicles Act protects a Good Samaritan who helps in good faith, and the rule grew out of a Supreme Court case brought by the SaveLIFE Foundation. In practice, here is what to do.

  1. Make yourself safe first. Switch on your hazard lights and stay clear of moving traffic.
  2. Call 112 and ask for the police and an ambulance. Give the exact place.
  3. Do not move the person unless there is immediate danger, such as fire. Moving someone with a neck or back injury can make it worse.
  4. Stay with them and keep them calm until help arrives.

The Act calls the first hour after a serious injury the "golden hour". Quick care in that hour gives the best chance of survival, so the call you make in the first minutes counts the most.

Know the rules where you live

Penalties and rules change, so do not rely on memory or on social media posts. The Delhi Traffic Police website lists current offences and challans. If you live in another state, check the traffic police site for your city.

If you want to bring a road safety session to your school, workplace or neighbourhood, contact us. You can also see more of our work in the Our work gallery, and young learners can study for free on our Study page.

Rules only work when people follow them even when nobody is watching. So the best habit you can build is the simplest one: treat every journey, short or long, as one that matters. Wear the helmet for the five-minute ride to the shop, too. Most crashes happen close to home, on roads we know well, and a moment of attention costs nothing.

Frequently asked questions about road safety awareness

Does a pillion rider also need a helmet?

Yes. The Motor Vehicles Act requires riders of two-wheelers, and their pillion passengers, to wear protective headgear that meets the Bureau of Indian Standards. A helmet only protects you when the strap is fastened.

If I help an accident victim, can the police trouble me?

The law protects you. Section 134A of the Motor Vehicles Act says a Good Samaritan who helps in good faith is not liable to civil or criminal action. A Good Samaritan who calls the police or takes the victim to hospital also cannot be forced to stay or answer unnecessary questions.

What is the golden hour?

The Motor Vehicles Act defines the golden hour as the first hour after a serious injury. During that hour, quick medical care gives the best chance of saving a life. That is why calling for help immediately matters so much.

Which number should I call after a crash?

Call 112, India's single emergency number, and ask for the police and an ambulance. Many states also run 108 for ambulances. Keep the line open and tell them the exact location.

What are the fines for traffic offences in Delhi?

Penalties change, so check the current official list rather than a blog. The Delhi Traffic Police website publishes the latest offences and amounts.
